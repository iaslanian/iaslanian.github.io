Context

Every job ends with a document. Customers couldn’t change theirs.

ServiceTitan runs the back office for trade businesses – HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing. Every job produces paperwork: an estimate before the work, an invoice after it, work orders and service agreements in between. That paper is what the homeowner actually holds, so for the business it’s part of the brand.

But the templates were rigid.

Moving a logo, adding a warranty clause or a signature line meant filing a ticket and waiting for our services team to edit the template by hand – often for weeks. Larger customers paid for custom templates; smaller ones lived with the default and printed whatever the system gave them.

Hi! Can we move our logo to the right and add a warranty line to our invoices?
Sure – I’ll open a request with our services team.
How long does that usually take?
Around two to three weeks.
…we’ll just keep printing the old ones then.

The stakes

Here, invoices aren’t paperwork. They are the business.

Two markets meet in one document. ServiceTitan earns from subscriptions and from the money its customers bill through the platform – so every dollar passes through an invoice or estimate. The problem

The most customer-facing thing we produced was the least in the customer’s control.

Talking to customers, support and the services team, the same four issues kept coming up:

01DependenceEvery small change went through support, so businesses stopped asking and kept documents they didn’t like.
02InconsistencyInvoices, estimates and agreements from the same company looked like they came from three different ones.
03Cost to serveCustom template requests ate services hours – work that grew with every new customer instead of scaling.
04Missed valueDocuments mattered to customers, yet they gave nobody a reason to upgrade.

So the design challenge became:

How might we let any office manager change a document in minutes – without breaking the job data that fills it?

The answer wasn’t another settings page. It was an editor.

How it started

A hackathon idea that won – and then had to become real

The project began as an initiative from our team, not a roadmap item. At the company hackathon, our product manager and I pitched a simple question: what if customers could build their own documents the way they build a slide in a presentation tool? In a few days we had a clickable prototype – tools on the left, the page in the middle, pages on the right – and a live demo of an invoice being assembled from blocks.

We won. More importantly, the win gave us a mandate and a small team – a PM, a squad of engineers and me as the only designer – to build Template Manager from scratch.

Research

Learning from the tools people already use

Before drawing anything, I went through the document builders our customers already knew: invoice editors inside accounting software, proposal tools like PandaDoc, design editors like Canva, and plain Google Docs. Office managers at trade businesses aren’t designers – but almost all of them had made a slide or edited a doc. That shaped three principles.

01Familiar over cleverReuse the slide-editor layout everyone knows instead of inventing a new mental model.
02Data, not textUnlike Canva, our documents are filled from job data. Every placeholder had to be a live field.
03Guardrails over freedomFree-form layouts break in print and on phones. Blocks snap into a flowing page.
Customer interviews

50+ conversations with the people who run the business

We ran more than 50 interviews with owners of multi-million-dollar trade businesses – companies with dozens of technicians and their own office teams. Each session had two halves. First, they walked us through how documents get made today, in ServiceTitan and everything around it: Word files, PDF editors, QuickBooks, printed forms with a logo taped on. Then we put the latest prototype in front of them and watched.

01Documents sellOwners saw the estimate as their pitch. A clean, branded estimate with clear options was how they won the job.
02Workarounds everywhereMany kept a second set of templates outside the platform and retyped job data into them by hand.
03Owners decide, office doesThe owner decided what a document should say – but someone in the office had to actually make the change.

The prototype changed with almost every round: we cut what owners ignored and doubled down on what they kept asking about.

The solution

Familiar layout, data-aware blocks

We didn’t invent a new interaction model. The editor borrows the three-panel layout office staff already know from slide tools – but every block is wired to job data. Drop Line items and you get the whole table, connected to the job; drop Signature and the technician can collect it on site.

123
1LeftBlocks and fieldsEverything you can put on a page: text, logo, customer info, line items, totals, terms, signature, pay-now button – plus a searchable list of merge fields.
2CenterThe pageA real letter-size page you edit directly. Blocks snap into a flowing layout, so the document stays readable on paper, on screen and on a phone.
3RightPagesThumbnails of every page – reorder, duplicate or add one for terms, photos or a warranty. Like slides, but for paperwork.
Trade-offs

Three calls that shaped the product

01Flowing page over a free canvasInstead ofFree-form drag-anywhere, like CanvaDocuments print, render on phones and grow with the number of line items. A free canvas looked great in the demo and broke on the first long invoice. Blocks that stack in a flow survive any job.
02Tokens over a formula languageInstead ofConditional logic and calculated fields in v1Power users asked for “if-then” rules. Office staff struggled with even raw placeholders. We shipped simple tokens with a fallback value and kept logic for later – it covered almost every real document.
03Versioning before launchInstead ofShip editing first, add history laterIt pushed the date back, but testing made it clear: without drafts and rollback, nobody would dare to touch a live invoice. Safety turned out to be the feature that unlocked all the others.

Key features

Three features that did the heavy lifting

Fields that fill themselvesAny text on the page can contain merge fields – customer name, job address, technician, total due. In edit mode they appear as blue tokens, so it’s obvious what’s data and what’s fixed copy. Preview swaps in a real job, so the office manager sees exactly what the homeowner will receive before publishing.
Start from a template, a blank page or your own PDFMost customers didn’t want to start from nothing. The entry point offers a ready-made template for each document type, a blank page for those who do, and a way to upload an existing document – the paper forms businesses had used for years – and add fields and a signature on top.

Usability testing

Tested with daily users, not buyers

Owners told us what a document should achieve, but they weren’t the ones building it. For usability testing we deliberately skipped owners and executives and recruited the people who live in the product every day: office managers, dispatchers, customer service reps and accounting staff.

They got real tasks on a working prototype – rebrand an invoice, add a warranty clause to an agreement, put a signature line on a work order, turn an old paper form into a fillable template – and we watched where they hesitated.

Testing insight
90%of 84 testers finished the task and published their first template
We worried a blank editor would scare people who had never designed anything. Instead, office staff treated it like the slide tools they already knew – clicks clustered on blocks, the drop zone and Publish, not on help.The hesitation came right before Publish. People weren’t afraid of the editor – they were afraid of breaking an invoice that was already going out to customers.

Results

Once beta customers got their hands on it, the conversation changed from “can you change this for us?” to “what else can it do?”. Template Manager was packaged into the paid subscription tier, and the value it added let the business raise the price of that tier. Template edits moved out of the support queue and into customers’ own hands.

Reflection

The best B2B features remove a phone call.

Being the only designer meant owning the whole arc – pitch, research, interaction model, specs and the beta loop. Two lessons stayed with me. A prototype beats a deck: the hackathon demo aligned product, engineering and leadership before a single spec existed.

And test with the people who do the work. Owners bought the product, but office staff decided whether it got used – and their fear of breaking a live invoice shaped the feature that mattered most.

Safety drove adoption more than any editing feature did.
